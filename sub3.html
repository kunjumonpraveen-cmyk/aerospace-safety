SUBJECT 03 · 01

What Is an Aircraft?

An aircraft is a vehicle capable of sustained or controlled flight through the atmosphere by using aerodynamic forces. The term includes many configurations, from small training aeroplanes and gliders to transport aircraft, helicopters and specialised unmanned vehicles. Although their shapes and propulsion systems differ, aircraft share a basic requirement: the vehicle must generate and control forces and moments that allow it to remain airborne and follow a desired flight path.

The atmosphere is therefore not merely the background in which an aircraft operates. It is part of the physical system. A wing, rotor or other lifting surface interacts with moving air to produce aerodynamic forces. Propulsion supplies energy and thrust, while the structure carries aerodynamic, inertial and operational loads. Flight controls change the orientation and magnitude of these forces so that the aircraft can climb, descend, turn, accelerate, decelerate or maintain a selected condition.

An aircraft can be understood as an integrated system rather than simply a flying machine. Its structure, propulsion, flight controls, electrical and hydraulic systems, avionics, landing gear, fuel system and human interfaces must work together. A failure that appears to belong to one subsystem can therefore affect the behaviour of the complete vehicle.

From a safety perspective, this systems view is fundamental. Safe aircraft operation depends on maintaining sufficient control authority, structural integrity, propulsion capability and situational awareness within the approved operating envelope. Understanding what an aircraft is provides the foundation for understanding why failures become hazardous.

SUBJECT 03 · 02

What Is a Spacecraft?

A spacecraft is a vehicle designed to operate beyond the conventional atmosphere, where aerodynamic flight is no longer the primary means of support or control. Spacecraft include satellites, crewed vehicles, robotic probes, orbital laboratories and vehicles used for transportation between different regions of space.

Unlike an aircraft, a spacecraft cannot normally rely on wings to remain aloft. Its motion is governed predominantly by gravity and orbital mechanics. Small propulsion systems are used to change velocity, alter attitude, perform rendezvous manoeuvres, maintain an orbit or initiate a departure or re-entry trajectory. Attitude-control systems orient the vehicle so that antennas, sensors, engines and thermal surfaces can perform their functions correctly.

The space environment imposes conditions that are unfamiliar to ordinary terrestrial vehicles. There is essentially no surrounding atmosphere for conventional cooling or aerodynamic control. Vacuum affects materials, lubricants, seals and human life support. Radiation can degrade electronics and materials. Large temperature differences can develop between illuminated and shaded surfaces. Micrometeoroids and orbital debris can create high-energy impact hazards.

A spacecraft is consequently a collection of tightly coupled subsystems: structure, propulsion, power, thermal control, communications, guidance and navigation, avionics, payloads and, for crewed vehicles, environmental control and life-support systems. Spacecraft safety depends on controlling failures across these interacting systems while retaining sufficient capability to complete the mission or protect the crew.

SUBJECT 03 · 03

Aeronautics and Astronautics

Aerospace engineering is commonly divided into aeronautics and astronautics. Aeronautics concerns vehicles operating within an atmosphere, while astronautics concerns vehicles and missions operating in space. The distinction is useful because the dominant physical phenomena in the two regimes are different, even though many engineering disciplines are shared.

Aeronautical vehicles depend strongly on atmospheric properties. Air density, pressure, temperature and viscosity influence lift, drag, propulsion performance and structural loads. Aircraft use aerodynamic surfaces, rotors or other devices to exchange momentum with the surrounding air. Their trajectories are consequently shaped by the interaction between vehicle motion and the atmosphere.

Astronautical vehicles operate primarily under gravitational and orbital mechanics. Once outside the dense atmosphere, aerodynamic forces become negligible for most missions. A spacecraft can therefore continue moving without continuously producing aerodynamic lift. Its trajectory is changed by applying relatively small changes in velocity at carefully selected times.

The two disciplines share structures, materials, propulsion, control engineering, electronics, software, human factors and systems engineering. However, the design margins and failure modes can be very different. A conventional aircraft may experience aerodynamic stall, icing and engine flameout; a spacecraft may face vacuum exposure, loss of attitude control, thermal imbalance or orbital collision risk.

For aerospace safety, understanding this distinction prevents inappropriate transfer of assumptions. A safety method developed for atmospheric flight may require substantial modification before it is applied to a spacecraft.

SUBJECT 03 · 04

Aircraft and Spacecraft: Fundamental Differences

Aircraft and spacecraft are both aerospace vehicles, but they operate under fundamentally different physical conditions. An aircraft normally depends on the atmosphere to generate lift and to provide a medium through which propulsion operates. A spacecraft normally operates where the atmosphere is extremely thin or absent and therefore relies on gravitational dynamics, propulsion and attitude control.

Aircraft are continuously exposed to aerodynamic forces. Increasing airspeed generally increases dynamic pressure, while changing angle of attack changes the aerodynamic forces acting on the vehicle. Spacecraft in orbit can experience almost no aerodynamic force, yet they remain in motion because orbital velocity and gravitational acceleration combine to produce a continuous free-fall trajectory.

The energy management problem is also different. An aircraft continuously exchanges energy between altitude, airspeed and propulsion. A spacecraft may use a relatively brief propulsion event to change its orbital energy, after which it can coast for a long period. A small velocity change can eventually produce a large change in orbital position.

Thermal behaviour is another major difference. Aircraft can reject substantial heat to the atmosphere through convection and can use aerodynamic airflow for cooling. Spacecraft primarily reject heat through radiation, making thermal design a critical subsystem.

These differences create different safety problems. Aircraft safety is strongly influenced by aerodynamic control, structural loads, propulsion reliability and the atmospheric environment. Spacecraft safety adds vacuum, radiation, orbital debris, long-duration autonomy and severe limitations on physical access and repair.

SUBJECT 03 · 05

The Earth's Atmosphere

Earth's atmosphere is the gaseous envelope surrounding the planet and is the operating medium for most aeronautical vehicles. It is composed primarily of nitrogen and oxygen with smaller quantities of argon, carbon dioxide, water vapour and other gases. Its properties vary with altitude, latitude, season, weather and local conditions.

For aerospace engineering, the atmosphere is important because it provides both useful and harmful effects. It provides the air through which wings and rotors generate aerodynamic forces and through which many engines obtain oxidant. At the same time, it produces drag, turbulence, icing, precipitation, wind shear and aerodynamic heating at high speed.

Atmospheric density decreases substantially with increasing altitude. This affects lift because aerodynamic force depends on dynamic pressure. At a given true airspeed, an aircraft operating at higher altitude generally encounters lower density and therefore produces less aerodynamic force for the same configuration. Engines also experience changes in performance because the mass flow of air changes with density.

The atmosphere is not a uniform fluid. Its temperature and pressure profiles influence aircraft performance, engine operation, altimetry and human physiology. Weather systems introduce additional variability that must be considered during flight planning.

The atmosphere also provides the medium through which an aircraft can dissipate heat and, during emergencies such as an engine failure, continue to generate aerodynamic lift. Understanding atmospheric behaviour is therefore fundamental to both aircraft performance and safety.

SUBJECT 03 · 06

Atmospheric Pressure, Density and Temperature

Pressure, density and temperature are three fundamental atmospheric properties that strongly influence aircraft behaviour. Pressure is the force exerted by the surrounding gas per unit area. Density describes the amount of mass contained in a given volume. Temperature represents the thermal state of the gas and affects both pressure-density relationships and the speed of sound.

For an idealised gas, pressure, density and temperature are related by the equation p = ρRT, where p is pressure, ρ is density, R is the specific gas constant and T is absolute temperature. This relationship is important because an aircraft does not respond simply to altitude; it responds to the actual properties of the air surrounding it.

Dynamic pressure is particularly important in flight. It is commonly expressed as q = 1/2 ρV², where V is true airspeed. Aerodynamic lift and drag are strongly related to dynamic pressure. If density falls, the aircraft generally needs greater true airspeed or a different aerodynamic configuration to produce the same force.

Temperature also influences engine performance and the speed of sound. In a hot atmosphere, density may be lower than under standard conditions at the same pressure altitude. This can increase take-off distance and reduce engine and aerodynamic performance.

These properties are therefore not academic quantities. They directly affect runway performance, climb capability, stall behaviour, engine output, indicated airspeed interpretation and high-altitude operations.

SUBJECT 03 · 07

Gravity and Weight

Gravity is the attractive force between masses. Near Earth's surface, gravitational acceleration is approximately 9.81 m/s², although its exact value varies slightly with location and altitude. In aircraft engineering, the force produced by gravity acting on the aircraft's mass is called weight.

The relationship is expressed as W = mg, where W is weight, m is mass and g is gravitational acceleration. Mass is an intrinsic measure of the amount of matter in the vehicle, whereas weight depends on the local gravitational field. This distinction becomes particularly important when comparing aircraft operations with spaceflight.

Aircraft must generate sufficient upward aerodynamic force to counteract their weight during steady level flight. If lift becomes less than weight without a corresponding change in trajectory, the aircraft begins to lose altitude. During manoeuvres, apparent loading can be much greater than the vehicle's static weight because acceleration produces additional forces.

Gravity also dominates spacecraft trajectories. An orbit is not an absence of gravity; rather, the spacecraft is continuously accelerating toward the central body while its tangential velocity causes it to keep missing the surface. This is why orbital flight is often described as continuous free fall.

Understanding gravity and weight is essential to analysing take-off, climb, descent, manoeuvring loads, payload limits, launch performance, orbital motion and re-entry.

SUBJECT 03 · 08

The Four Forces of Flight

The classical four-force model describes the major forces acting on a conventional aircraft in flight: lift, weight, thrust and drag. Lift is the aerodynamic force generally acting perpendicular to the relative airflow; weight acts toward the centre of gravity and ultimately toward Earth; thrust is produced by the propulsion system; and drag opposes motion through the air.

The four forces are not always equal. In steady, level, unaccelerated flight, lift approximately balances weight and thrust approximately balances drag. This does not mean that the aircraft is permanently locked into those values. During acceleration, climb, descent and turning, the magnitude and direction of the forces change.

For example, during a climb the propulsion system may provide additional energy while the aircraft's trajectory converts some kinetic energy into gravitational potential energy. During a descent, gravitational potential energy can be converted into airspeed. During a turn, the aircraft must generate an aerodynamic force with a horizontal component to provide the required centripetal acceleration.

The four-force model is therefore a simplified way of visualising aircraft energy and motion. It is not a complete aircraft dynamics model because real aircraft also experience moments, side forces and numerous secondary aerodynamic effects.

For safety engineering, the model is valuable because many hazardous conditions can be interpreted as an imbalance or loss of one of the fundamental capabilities: inadequate lift, excessive drag, insufficient thrust or excessive weight/load. It provides a simple physical framework for understanding more complex failures.

SUBJECT 03 · 09

Lift: Fundamental Concept

Lift is the aerodynamic force produced when a body moves through a fluid and is oriented so that the resulting pressure and momentum changes produce a force component perpendicular to the relative airflow. For an aircraft wing, lift is generated by the interaction between wing geometry, angle of attack, airspeed, air density and the surrounding flow field.

A commonly used engineering relationship is L = 1/2 ρV²SCL, where L is lift, ρ is air density, V is true airspeed, S is reference wing area and CL is the lift coefficient. The equation shows that lift is influenced by both flight conditions and aircraft configuration.

Angle of attack is particularly important. As angle of attack increases within the normal operating range, lift generally increases. Beyond a critical angle, the airflow can separate substantially from the upper surface and lift can decrease rapidly. This condition is associated with aerodynamic stall.

Lift is not simply a matter of air “pushing upward.” The wing creates a pressure distribution and changes the momentum of the airflow. The resulting aerodynamic force can be analysed using both pressure and momentum principles.

The safety significance is substantial. Insufficient lift can result from low airspeed, excessive angle of attack, high aircraft weight, low density or configuration problems. Understanding the mechanism is necessary before hazards such as stall, icing, contamination or high-altitude performance limitations can be analysed.

SUBJECT 03 · 10

Drag: Fundamental Concept

Drag is the component of aerodynamic force that acts opposite to the vehicle's direction of motion through the surrounding fluid. It is not a single phenomenon. Aerodynamic drag includes several components, with parasitic drag and induced drag being particularly important for conventional aircraft.

Parasitic drag arises from the aircraft moving through the air and includes form drag, skin-friction drag and interference effects. It generally increases strongly with airspeed. Induced drag is associated with the generation of lift and is particularly significant at low speeds and high lift coefficients. At high Mach numbers, compressibility and shock waves can introduce additional wave drag.

The total drag of an aircraft therefore changes with flight condition. There is typically a speed region where total drag is relatively low and another region where high-speed drag begins to rise sharply. Aircraft performance calculations use this behaviour to determine efficient climb, cruise and range conditions.

Propulsion must overcome drag to maintain steady flight. If available thrust falls below the drag required at a particular condition, the aircraft cannot maintain that condition without changing speed, altitude or configuration.

From a safety perspective, drag becomes important during engine-out flight, icing, landing configuration, emergency descents and high-speed operation. A change in drag can alter glide performance, fuel consumption and the amount of thrust required to maintain control.

SUBJECT 03 · 11

Thrust and Propulsion

Thrust is the force generated by an aircraft propulsion system to accelerate the aircraft or to counter aerodynamic drag. Propulsion systems achieve this by exchanging momentum with a surrounding fluid or by accelerating exhaust products. Common aircraft propulsion systems include piston-propeller combinations, turboprops, turbojets, turbofans and electric propulsion systems.

A propulsion system contains more than its visible engine. It may include air intakes, compressors, combustion systems, turbines, exhaust systems, gearboxes, propellers, fuel systems, control systems and supporting electrical or hydraulic equipment. The performance of the complete propulsion system determines how much useful thrust or shaft power is available.

Jet engines operate by ingesting air, compressing it, adding energy through combustion or another process, and accelerating the resulting flow. Propellers produce thrust by accelerating a larger mass of air by a smaller velocity increment. These mechanisms lead to different efficiency and operating characteristics.

Thrust is not constant. It varies with altitude, temperature, airspeed, engine condition and power setting. A high-altitude aircraft may have less available thrust than it has at sea level. Engine degradation, inlet distortion, icing or fuel problems can also reduce performance.

Safety therefore depends on understanding both thrust production and the barriers against propulsion failures. Engine redundancy, fuel management, monitoring, maintenance, fire protection and emergency procedures all depend on the physical behaviour of the propulsion system.

SUBJECT 03 · 12

Aircraft Weight and Mass

Aircraft mass is the amount of matter contained in the aircraft, including structure, occupants, fuel, equipment and payload. Weight is the gravitational force acting on that mass. The distinction is important because aircraft performance calculations use mass and weight in different ways.

An aircraft's operating mass changes throughout a flight. Fuel is consumed continuously, reducing mass and therefore weight. Payload configuration can change the centre of gravity and the total operating weight. Equipment additions or modifications can also alter mass properties.

Maximum take-off mass or weight is established from structural, aerodynamic, runway, propulsion and performance considerations. Exceeding approved limits can increase take-off distance, reduce climb performance, increase structural loads and alter stall characteristics.

Weight also affects landing performance. A heavier aircraft generally requires greater lift and therefore higher speeds or a different configuration for the same aerodynamic condition. The additional kinetic energy associated with a higher landing mass can increase braking and stopping requirements.

From a safety perspective, accurate weight control is essential. Incorrect loading data, fuel quantity errors, cargo misdistribution and inaccurate assumptions about passenger or equipment mass can produce a vehicle condition different from that assumed during performance calculations. Weight control is therefore both a technical and operational safety function.

SUBJECT 03 · 13

Balance and Centre of Gravity

The centre of gravity, or CG, is the point through which the resultant weight of an aircraft can be considered to act. Its position depends on the distribution of mass throughout the vehicle. Fuel, passengers, cargo, equipment and structural modifications can all change the CG location.

Aircraft are designed with an approved centre-of-gravity envelope. If the CG moves too far forward, the aircraft may require excessive control force or stabilising authority and can experience degraded take-off, landing or trim performance. If it moves too far aft, longitudinal stability and recovery characteristics can deteriorate.

The CG also affects the amount of lift or tail force required to maintain equilibrium. The aircraft is not simply a rigid object with one force acting on it; aerodynamic forces act at different locations and create moments. Safe flight requires the moments about the CG to be appropriately balanced.

Loading procedures therefore form part of the aircraft's safety system. Cargo must be placed in approved compartments and passengers may need to be distributed according to the loading plan. Fuel transfer can also change CG during flight.

Understanding CG is especially important because an aircraft can have fully functioning engines, controls and structure and still become unsafe if its mass distribution is outside the approved envelope.

SUBJECT 03 · 14

Stability and Control

Stability describes how an aircraft responds to disturbances, while control refers to the ability of the pilot or automatic system to deliberately change the aircraft's attitude and flight path. The two concepts are related but not identical. A stable aircraft tends to resist or recover from disturbances; a controllable aircraft can respond to commanded inputs.

Aircraft motion is commonly described using three rotational axes: longitudinal roll, lateral pitch and vertical yaw. Ailerons primarily influence roll, elevators or stabilators influence pitch, and rudders influence yaw, although real aircraft use coupled aerodynamic and control effects.

Stability can be considered longitudinal, lateral and directional. The location of the centre of gravity, wing geometry, tail configuration and aerodynamic characteristics all influence stability. Modern aircraft may deliberately use relaxed natural stability and rely heavily on flight-control computers.

Control authority is not unlimited. At very low airspeed, aerodynamic control surfaces may become less effective. At high speed, structural and aerodynamic limitations may restrict manoeuvring. A flight-control system can also suffer from sensor, actuator, power, software or communication failures.

Safety analysis therefore considers both inherent vehicle stability and the availability of control authority after failures. Redundancy, control-law design, mechanical backup, actuator architecture and pilot interfaces are all developed around this fundamental relationship.

SUBJECT 03 · 15

Aircraft Flight Envelope

The flight envelope is the range of speed, altitude, load factor, configuration and other conditions within which an aircraft is designed and approved to operate. It represents the boundaries imposed by aerodynamic, structural, propulsion and controllability limitations.

One important boundary is the low-speed limit. As airspeed decreases, an aircraft may need to increase angle of attack to maintain lift. Eventually the critical angle of attack can be exceeded and the wing may stall. At the high-speed end, compressibility, shock waves, structural loads and flutter can impose restrictions.

Load factor is another major consideration. Manoeuvring or turbulence can produce forces several times the aircraft's static weight. The structural design must withstand specified combinations of speed and load factor. Operating outside those limits can produce permanent deformation or structural failure.

Altitude also changes the envelope. Engine thrust, aerodynamic performance, pressurisation requirements and available oxygen all vary with altitude. Certain aircraft have a maximum operating altitude beyond which performance or structural margins become inadequate.

The flight envelope is therefore a safety boundary, not merely a performance chart. Flight crew procedures, warning systems, automatic protections and certification requirements are designed to keep the aircraft away from conditions in which its structural or aerodynamic margins could be lost.

SUBJECT 03 · 16

Speed, Mach Number and Altitude

Aerospace vehicles operate over a wide range of speeds and altitudes, and the meaning of speed changes with the surrounding environment. Airspeed can be represented in several ways because pilots and engineers need different information about the aircraft's aerodynamic condition, energy state and position.

Mach number is the ratio of an object's speed to the local speed of sound. Because the speed of sound changes primarily with temperature, the same true airspeed can correspond to different Mach numbers under different atmospheric conditions. At sufficiently high Mach numbers, compressibility effects become important.

Altitude also affects aircraft performance. As altitude increases, atmospheric density generally decreases. This can reduce aerodynamic force for a given true airspeed and change engine performance. Aircraft may therefore use higher true airspeeds at altitude while maintaining a particular indicated aerodynamic condition.

The interaction between speed and altitude defines important operational limits. High-speed flight can produce structural and thermal problems, while low-speed flight can approach stall margins. High altitude can improve cruise efficiency but may reduce available thrust and leave smaller margins between operating conditions and physiological or aerodynamic limits.

Understanding speed, Mach number and altitude is therefore essential to performance planning, navigation, structural protection and safe operation.

SUBJECT 03 · 17

Climb, Cruise and Descent

A flight profile is a controlled sequence in which the aircraft manages altitude, airspeed, energy and configuration. The broad phases of climb, cruise and descent illustrate how the balance between aerodynamic forces, propulsion and gravity changes during normal operation.

During climb, the aircraft gains gravitational potential energy. Some of the propulsion system's available energy is therefore used to overcome the component of weight associated with the flight path, while the aircraft must still overcome aerodynamic drag. Climb performance depends on aircraft weight, altitude, temperature, engine output and configuration.

Cruise is generally selected to provide efficient progress over the route. The aircraft may operate at a nearly constant altitude and speed, with thrust approximately balancing drag. Small changes in weight and atmospheric conditions require continual adjustments.

During descent, gravitational potential energy is converted into kinetic energy and aerodynamic dissipation. The aircraft can descend with relatively low engine power while controlling airspeed through pitch, configuration and drag devices. An emergency descent may use a substantially different profile because the priority may be reaching a lower breathable altitude rapidly.

These phases demonstrate why aerospace safety is an energy-management problem as well as a control problem. A safe flight requires sufficient margins for speed, altitude, fuel, thrust and controllability throughout the transition between phases.

SUBJECT 03 · 18

Approaching the Edge of the Atmosphere

As an aircraft climbs to increasingly high altitude, the atmosphere becomes progressively thinner. Aerodynamic forces decrease, engine performance changes and the assumptions used for conventional aircraft operation become less effective. At sufficiently high altitude, orbital mechanics begin to become more important than ordinary aerodynamic flight.

The transition is gradual rather than a sharp boundary. There is no single physical altitude at which “the atmosphere ends.” Even at hundreds of kilometres above Earth, a very thin atmosphere remains. However, density becomes low enough that conventional aerodynamic lift and drag are extremely small for many spacecraft.

For a conventional aircraft, declining density means that a higher true airspeed may be required to produce the same lift. Propulsion systems also face reduced air mass flow. At the same time, human physiological constraints become severe because pressure and oxygen availability fall.

Launch vehicles exploit this changing environment deliberately. They begin in dense atmosphere, where aerodynamic drag and heating are significant, and progressively transition to a regime in which most of the vehicle's performance is governed by gravitational and orbital mechanics.

Understanding this transition helps explain why aerospace vehicles are designed differently for aircraft flight, high-altitude operation, launch and spaceflight.

SUBJECT 03 · 19

The Space Environment

The space environment differs profoundly from the terrestrial environment. A spacecraft may experience vacuum, radiation, extreme thermal cycling, atomic oxygen in low Earth orbit, micrometeoroids, orbital debris and long periods without physical access for maintenance.

Vacuum eliminates normal atmospheric pressure and greatly reduces convective heat transfer. Materials and lubricants selected for terrestrial equipment may behave differently under vacuum. Trapped gases can outgas, seals can lose function and some mechanisms require specialised materials or lubrication.

Radiation can affect both humans and electronics. High-energy particles may produce cumulative dose, single-event effects or degradation of semiconductor devices. Spacecraft therefore use shielding, component selection, fault tolerance and operational strategies to manage radiation risk.

Thermal conditions can also be severe. A surface exposed directly to sunlight may become much hotter than a surface facing deep space. Because heat transfer is dominated by radiation, spacecraft thermal control depends on coatings, insulation, radiators, heat pipes and controlled orientation.

Micrometeoroids and debris create impact hazards. Even small objects can possess substantial kinetic energy at orbital velocity. Protection can involve shielding, tracking, avoidance manoeuvres and structural redundancy.

These environmental factors form the baseline against which spacecraft systems must be designed and maintained.

SUBJECT 03 · 20

Vacuum and Microgravity

Vacuum means that the surrounding pressure is extremely low compared with Earth's atmosphere. It changes how heat transfer, fluid behaviour, combustion, materials and human physiology operate. In crewed spacecraft, maintaining an internal pressure environment is therefore a primary life-safety requirement.

In vacuum, conventional combustion cannot continue without a supplied oxidiser. External convective cooling is also essentially unavailable. Liquids behave differently because surface tension becomes dominant and gas-liquid separation becomes more difficult. Materials may release trapped gases through outgassing.

Microgravity is commonly experienced by spacecraft in orbit because the vehicle and its occupants are in continuous free fall. The term does not mean that gravity has disappeared. Earth’s gravity remains substantial in low orbit; the apparent weightlessness results from the shared free-fall motion.

Microgravity changes how people move, how fluids distribute, how dust behaves and how mechanical systems are lubricated and operated. Long-duration human exposure can also produce physiological effects that require exercise, medical monitoring and life-support strategies.

These conditions demonstrate why spacecraft cannot simply be treated as aircraft without wings. Their systems must be designed around a vacuum environment and prolonged free-fall conditions.

SUBJECT 03 · 21

Orbital Motion

An orbit is a trajectory produced when an object moves under gravitational influence with sufficient tangential velocity that it continually falls toward a central body without striking it. A spacecraft in orbit is therefore not escaping gravity; it is continuously responding to gravity.

Imagine launching a vehicle horizontally from high above Earth. If its horizontal velocity is low, it falls back toward the surface. If velocity is increased, the trajectory extends farther before returning. At an appropriate velocity and altitude, the curvature of the trajectory matches the curvature of Earth closely enough to produce an orbit.

In a circular orbit, gravitational acceleration provides the required centripetal acceleration. The spacecraft continuously changes direction even though its speed may remain approximately constant. In an elliptical orbit, speed changes along the orbit: the spacecraft moves faster near the point closest to Earth and slower farther away.

Propulsive manoeuvres alter orbital energy and momentum. A relatively small change in velocity, known as delta-v, can change altitude, orbital period, inclination or encounter geometry.

Orbital mechanics is fundamental to spacecraft safety because incorrect velocity, attitude or timing can result in missed rendezvous, undesirable trajectories, collision risk or loss of mission capability.

SUBJECT 03 · 22

Orbit, Altitude and Velocity

Orbital altitude and velocity are closely related through gravitational mechanics. For an ideal circular orbit around a spherical body, orbital velocity depends primarily on the gravitational parameter of the central body and the orbital radius. The simplified relationship is v = √(μ/r), where μ is the standard gravitational parameter and r is the distance from the centre of the body.

This relationship shows that a spacecraft in a higher circular orbit generally requires a lower orbital velocity than one in a lower circular orbit. However, reaching the higher orbit requires an appropriate change in orbital energy, and orbital period becomes longer as orbital radius increases.

Orbital velocity is not the same as a spacecraft's speed relative to the ground. A spacecraft's velocity is a vector with both magnitude and direction, while its ground track depends on Earth's rotation, orbit inclination and observation geometry.

Changing velocity at a particular point in an orbit changes the future trajectory. A short propulsion event can raise one side of an orbit, lower another side, change inclination or establish an intercept trajectory.

For safety engineering, the important point is that spacecraft position cannot be managed independently of velocity and time. Navigation errors, propulsion errors and timing errors can accumulate into significant trajectory deviations.

SUBJECT 03 · 23

Launch and Ascent

Launch is the process of accelerating a vehicle from the Earth's surface or another starting point into a trajectory that provides the required orbital or escape energy. It is one of the most demanding phases of aerospace operation because the vehicle passes rapidly through changing aerodynamic, thermal, structural and propulsion environments.

A launch vehicle must generate thrust greater than the effective opposing forces while maintaining a controlled trajectory. Near the surface, atmospheric drag is significant and aerodynamic loads can become severe. As altitude increases, atmospheric density falls and the vehicle can accelerate more efficiently toward its required orbital velocity.

Multi-stage rockets divide the vehicle into sections that can be discarded after their propellant is consumed. This improves mass efficiency because the vehicle does not have to continue accelerating empty tanks and engines that are no longer required.

Guidance and control systems continuously compare the desired trajectory with the vehicle's measured state. Engines or thrust-vectoring systems then make corrections. Structural loads, vibration, acoustic energy, thermal effects and propulsion transients all have to remain within design limits.

Launch safety therefore depends on propulsion reliability, structural integrity, guidance, range safety, telemetry, fault detection, abort strategies where applicable and rigorous pre-launch verification.

SUBJECT 03 · 24

Re-entry and Atmospheric Heating

Re-entry occurs when a spacecraft or other high-energy vehicle returns from space into an atmosphere. The vehicle's orbital or trajectory energy is transformed through aerodynamic deceleration, producing intense heating and large mechanical loads.

The heating is not simply caused by friction in the everyday sense. As the vehicle moves at very high speed, the air ahead of it is compressed strongly and its temperature rises. Shock waves and high-temperature gas effects create severe thermal loads around the vehicle, especially at leading edges and other high-heating regions.

The vehicle must manage both deceleration and heat. A blunt re-entry shape can create a strong shock wave and spread heating over a larger area, while thermal protection systems prevent excessive heat from reaching the primary structure or crew compartment.

Attitude is critical. A small change in orientation can substantially alter aerodynamic forces and heating. Guidance systems therefore maintain a controlled trajectory through the atmosphere. The vehicle must also survive rapidly changing pressure, aerodynamic loads and possible communication interruptions.

Re-entry is a major aerospace safety challenge because the vehicle is transitioning from an environment in which aerodynamic forces are minimal to one in which those forces rapidly become dominant.

SUBJECT 03 · 25

Spacecraft Structure

The spacecraft structure is the load-bearing framework that supports equipment, payloads, propulsion systems and, in crewed vehicles, the pressure vessel and occupants. It must survive launch loads, vibration, thermal cycling, deployment forces and the long-term space environment.

During launch, the structure can experience high acceleration, acoustic loads and vibration. Engines and turbopumps generate dynamic forces that are transmitted through the vehicle. Payloads and internal equipment have their own mass and natural frequencies, so structural design must prevent damaging resonance or excessive deformation.

A spacecraft structure may include primary load paths, secondary panels, equipment decks, pressure shells, trusses and attachment points. Materials are selected for strength, stiffness, mass efficiency, thermal behaviour, radiation resistance and compatibility with the space environment.

Structural margins are important because a spacecraft may be impossible to repair after launch. Designers therefore use analysis, qualification testing, inspection and manufacturing controls to establish confidence in the load-bearing system.

Structural failure can have cascading consequences. Loss of a pressure vessel can threaten a crew, while deformation can misalign sensors, jam mechanisms, damage wiring or prevent solar arrays from deploying. Structural integrity is therefore a system-level safety requirement.

SUBJECT 03 · 26

Spacecraft Propulsion

Spacecraft propulsion provides controlled changes in velocity, known as delta-v, allowing a vehicle to modify its orbit or trajectory. Propulsion is therefore less about continuously producing lift and more about changing the spacecraft's state at selected points in its mission.

Chemical propulsion systems generate high thrust by releasing stored chemical energy and accelerating exhaust. They are useful when large velocity changes must be achieved quickly, such as launch, major orbital manoeuvres or some landing operations. Electric propulsion systems produce much lower thrust but can achieve very high exhaust velocity and therefore excellent propellant efficiency over long operating periods.

Attitude-control propulsion may use small thrusters to rotate or stabilise the spacecraft. Reaction wheels and control moment gyros can perform some attitude-control functions without expelling propellant.

Propulsion systems introduce hazards including high-pressure fluids, energetic propellants, combustion instability, valve failure, leakage, contamination and unwanted thrust. Spacecraft may also become unsafe if an engine fires at the wrong time or in the wrong direction.

Propulsion safety therefore requires accurate command logic, isolation, fault detection, verification of firing conditions and robust guidance and navigation.

SUBJECT 03 · 27

Power and Energy Systems

Spacecraft require electrical energy to operate avionics, sensors, communications, propulsion controls, thermal systems and payloads. Unlike many terrestrial vehicles, a spacecraft may have limited opportunities to obtain or replace energy after launch.

Solar arrays are widely used to convert sunlight into electrical power. Batteries store energy for periods when sunlight is unavailable, such as orbital eclipse. Power-conditioning and distribution equipment regulates voltage and routes energy to the spacecraft's various loads.

The electrical system must be designed around generation capacity, storage capacity and peak demand. A spacecraft can experience both power shortage and excess generation. Either condition can damage equipment or reduce mission capability.

Power systems also interact with thermal control because electrical equipment converts some input energy into heat. A failed power component can therefore produce secondary thermal or avionics problems.

Safety features may include redundant power paths, current limiting, circuit protection, fault isolation and carefully controlled load shedding. Understanding the power architecture is essential because the loss of electricity can disable multiple seemingly unrelated spacecraft functions simultaneously.

SUBJECT 03 · 28

Thermal Control

Thermal control keeps aerospace systems within allowable temperature ranges. Temperature affects material strength, electronics, batteries, lubricants, sensors, propulsion systems and human survival. In spacecraft, thermal control is particularly challenging because there is almost no surrounding atmosphere to carry heat away by convection.

Spacecraft can receive solar radiation, planetary infrared radiation and heat generated internally by electrical and mechanical equipment. Heat must then be transported to suitable radiating surfaces and emitted into space.

Thermal-control methods include surface coatings, multilayer insulation, heat pipes, thermal straps, radiators, heaters and controlled spacecraft orientation. Some components may need active heating during cold conditions, while others need substantial heat rejection.

Aircraft have different thermal mechanisms because air can carry heat away and engines are surrounded by a moving fluid. High-speed aircraft also experience aerodynamic heating, while spacecraft experience long-duration radiative heating and cooling cycles.

Thermal failure can be gradual or sudden. Excessive temperature can degrade electronics, reduce battery life, distort structures or create fire hazards in crewed environments. Thermal design is therefore an essential safety barrier rather than simply a comfort feature.

SUBJECT 03 · 29

Communication and Navigation

Aerospace vehicles must know their state and, depending on mission architecture, must exchange information with other systems. Communication transfers commands, telemetry and mission data. Navigation determines position, velocity, attitude or trajectory relative to a reference frame.

Aircraft commonly use combinations of inertial systems, satellite navigation, radio navigation, air-data sensors and external surveillance. Spacecraft may use inertial sensors, star trackers, ground tracking, ranging, optical navigation and other specialised techniques.

Communication links can be affected by distance, antenna orientation, atmospheric conditions, interference, equipment failure and spacecraft attitude. Navigation errors can arise from sensor bias, drift, incorrect models or loss of reference signals.

The distinction between communication and navigation is important. A vehicle may lose communication with a ground station while continuing to navigate correctly, or it may retain communication while its navigation solution becomes unreliable.

Safety-critical aerospace systems therefore use redundancy, cross-checking, fault detection and carefully defined degraded modes. A loss of information can be hazardous, but an incorrect measurement can sometimes be more dangerous because the system may act confidently on false information.

SUBJECT 03 · 30

Life-Support and Crew Systems

Crewed aerospace vehicles must create an artificial environment that supports human life. The basic functions include maintaining breathable atmosphere, pressure, temperature, humidity and carbon-dioxide control while managing water, waste, food, fire risk and other consumables.

The cabin or habitat pressure must remain within an acceptable range. Oxygen concentration and partial pressure must support human metabolism without creating unnecessary fire risk. Carbon dioxide produced by occupants must be removed continuously. Trace contaminants may also require filtration.

Thermal control prevents both overheating and excessive cooling. Humidity must be managed because condensation can damage equipment and encourage biological growth. Water recovery and waste management become increasingly important during long-duration missions.

Crew systems also include restraints, seats, displays, emergency equipment, medical provisions and escape or abort systems where the vehicle architecture permits them.

The key safety principle is that human life depends on multiple coupled systems. A power failure can disable environmental control; loss of cooling can affect electronics; a pressure leak can create an immediate emergency. Crewed spacecraft therefore use monitoring, redundancy, isolation and emergency procedures to preserve survivable conditions.

SUBJECT 03 · 31

Space Suits and Human Survival

A spacesuit is effectively a small personal spacecraft. It must provide pressure, oxygen, carbon-dioxide removal, thermal regulation, communication and protection while allowing the wearer to move and perform useful work.

In vacuum, an unprotected human cannot survive because body fluids and tissues require a controlled pressure environment and the lungs cannot obtain oxygen from the surroundings. The suit therefore maintains a pressure environment around the body. Oxygen is supplied through a controlled system and carbon dioxide must be removed.

Mobility is a major engineering challenge. A pressurised suit resists movement, so joints, gloves and bearings must be designed to allow useful motion. Thermal control is also required because the suit may experience intense sunlight and deep-space cold while producing metabolic heat internally.

A portable life-support system can contain oxygen supplies, carbon-dioxide removal, cooling equipment, electrical power and communication equipment. The suit must also protect against small particles and other environmental hazards depending on the mission.

Spacesuit safety depends on pressure integrity, life-support capacity, thermal control, communications and procedures. A small leak or failure can become life-threatening quickly, so detection and contingency capability are essential.

SUBJECT 03 · 32

Crewed vs Uncrewed Vehicles

The presence of humans fundamentally changes aerospace system requirements. An uncrewed vehicle may be lost with significant financial or scientific consequences, but a crewed vehicle must preserve human life under a wide range of failures and emergencies.

Human-rated vehicles require environmental control, pressure integrity, life support, crew restraints, emergency equipment and appropriate escape or abort strategies. Their design must also consider human tolerance to acceleration, vibration, temperature, radiation, noise and workload.

Uncrewed vehicles can sometimes tolerate greater levels of environmental exposure because the payload does not require a breathable atmosphere or continuous human support. They may also be able to operate for long periods autonomously.

However, uncrewed systems are not automatically simple or low-risk. A satellite may be impossible to repair after launch and may depend on highly reliable software, power, communication and attitude-control systems. An autonomous aircraft can also create new hazards because decisions are made by software rather than directly by a pilot.

The safety philosophy therefore changes with mission consequence. Human-rated systems generally require exceptionally strong fault tolerance and survival barriers, while uncrewed systems place greater emphasis on mission assurance, autonomous recovery and protection of valuable assets.

SUBJECT 03 · 33

Aircraft, Spacecraft and Hybrid Vehicles

Some aerospace vehicles are designed to operate in more than one environment. Spaceplanes and other hybrid architectures combine characteristics of aircraft and spacecraft, creating a transition between aerodynamic and spaceflight regimes.

During atmospheric flight, such a vehicle may use wings, aerodynamic control surfaces and atmospheric propulsion. During spaceflight, it relies on spacecraft systems, orbital mechanics and reaction-control or rocket propulsion. Re-entry reverses the sequence and requires the vehicle to transition from spaceflight to controlled atmospheric flight.

The challenge is not simply combining two sets of hardware. The vehicle must remain controllable as the dominant physical forces change. Aerodynamic surfaces become ineffective in near-vacuum, while spacecraft propulsion and attitude systems become increasingly important. During re-entry, aerodynamic forces grow rapidly and the vehicle must tolerate high heating and deceleration.

Hybrid vehicles therefore have complex certification and safety requirements. Interfaces between different operating modes can become failure points. Software and control systems must know which regime the vehicle is in and apply appropriate guidance and control laws.

Understanding hybrid vehicles reinforces a central aerospace principle: safety depends on correctly managing transitions between physical environments as much as it depends on managing steady-state operation.

SUBJECT 03 · 34

Why Aerospace Vehicles Are Fundamentally Different

Aerospace vehicles operate with unusually high energy, tight performance margins and complex interactions between physical systems. Their failures can develop rapidly, occur far from maintenance facilities and affect large numbers of people or high-value assets.

An aircraft at altitude possesses significant kinetic and potential energy. A spacecraft in orbit possesses enormous orbital energy relative to the ground. Launch vehicles contain large quantities of chemical energy and must control that energy during acceleration. Re-entry vehicles must dissipate orbital energy safely through controlled atmospheric interaction.

Aerospace systems are also highly interconnected. Propulsion affects power, thermal behaviour and structural loads. Navigation affects guidance, which affects propulsion commands. Electrical failures can disable multiple systems. A software error can influence physical actuators and therefore change the vehicle's trajectory.

The environment adds further difficulty. Turbulence, icing and weather affect aircraft; vacuum, radiation and thermal extremes affect spacecraft. Many aerospace vehicles cannot be physically accessed once they are operating, so faults must often be detected and managed remotely or autonomously.

These characteristics explain why aerospace engineering relies heavily on redundancy, fault tolerance, verification, simulation, certification, configuration control, maintenance and rigorous operational procedures.

SUBJECT 03 · 35

The Aerospace Operating Environment

The aerospace operating environment is not a single environment. It is a sequence of conditions that can include ground operations, take-off, dense atmosphere, high altitude, near-space, orbital space, re-entry and landing. Each stage changes the physical forces and hazards acting on the vehicle.

Near the ground, aircraft experience strong aerodynamic forces, runway interaction, weather and human activity. During climb, pressure and density decrease while propulsion and performance change. At high altitude, the atmosphere becomes thinner and physiological constraints become increasingly important.

Launch vehicles experience especially rapid transitions. They begin under Earth's gravity and dense atmosphere, encounter changing aerodynamic loads and eventually enter a regime dominated by orbital mechanics. Re-entry reverses many of these conditions and introduces severe aerodynamic heating and deceleration.

Spacecraft may spend months or years in a vacuum environment, exposed to radiation and thermal cycling. They must continue operating without conventional maintenance access and often depend on autonomous fault management.

Safety engineering therefore begins with environmental definition. A hazard that is acceptable in one regime may be catastrophic in another. Correctly identifying the operating environment is one of the first steps in determining credible failure modes and effective safety barriers.

SUBJECT 03 · 36

Why These Fundamentals Matter to Aerospace Safety

Aerospace safety cannot be separated from the physical behaviour of the vehicle. Before a hazard can be identified, an engineer must understand what the vehicle is intended to do, which forces make that possible, what energy it contains and what environmental conditions surround it.

Consider a loss of engine thrust. Without understanding lift, drag, weight and flight energy, it is difficult to determine whether the aircraft can glide safely, how far it can travel or which control actions are required. Similarly, without understanding orbital mechanics, a spacecraft propulsion failure cannot be evaluated simply by asking whether the engine has stopped; the effect depends on the vehicle's current orbit, velocity and mission trajectory.

The same principle applies to spacecraft thermal, power, communication and life-support systems. Safety analysis requires knowledge of how these systems interact and which functions are essential to maintaining a safe state.

These fundamentals therefore provide the vocabulary for later safety engineering subjects: hazard identification, risk assessment, failure analysis, fault trees, FMEA, human factors, emergency response, maintenance safety, safety management systems and accident investigation.

A strong aerospace safety professional does not memorise isolated hazards. The professional understands the physical system well enough to predict where hazards can arise, how failures can propagate and which barriers can prevent an unsafe condition from becoming an accident.
