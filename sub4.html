SUBJECT 04 · 01

What Is an Aerospace Hazard?

An aerospace hazard is a condition, event, substance, energy source or system characteristic that has the potential to cause injury, death, environmental damage, equipment loss or unacceptable mission consequences. The word potential is important: a hazard does not have to produce an accident to be a hazard. A leaking fuel line, exposed electrical energy, unstable flight condition or incorrect navigation data can exist without causing harm if effective barriers prevent the hazardous energy from reaching a vulnerable target.

Hazards arise from both normal operations and failures. A rotating rotor is a normal operating feature, but its kinetic energy creates a severe hazard to people near the aircraft. Pressurised oxygen is necessary for life support, yet it can increase fire severity. A launch vehicle's propellant is essential to its mission while simultaneously representing an enormous stored-energy hazard.

Safety engineering therefore begins by identifying the hazard and understanding its mechanism. The engineer asks what energy or condition exists, what can release or change it, who or what can be exposed, and what sequence could turn the condition into an accident. Hazard identification is consequently a technical activity, not simply a checklist exercise.

SUBJECT 04 · 02

Hazard, Risk, Threat and Accident — Key Differences

Hazard, risk, threat and accident are related terms but they describe different things. A hazard is a source or condition with the potential to cause harm. Risk combines the possibility of an unwanted outcome with the severity of its consequences. A threat is a circumstance or agent capable of initiating or exploiting a hazardous condition. An accident is an actual undesired event that produces defined harmful consequences.

For example, aviation fuel is a hazardous substance because it can burn. The presence of an ignition source near a fuel vapour cloud creates a particular exposure scenario. The probability of ignition and the resulting consequences determine the risk. If ignition actually occurs and produces an uncontrolled fire, the event has moved from potential hazard to an accident or serious incident depending on the outcome.

This distinction prevents weak safety reasoning. Removing a hazard is not the same as reducing risk, and reducing probability is not the same as reducing consequence. Safety engineers need to identify the source, understand the initiating events, estimate credible outcomes and determine whether existing controls provide sufficient protection.

Clear terminology also improves communication between designers, operators, maintainers, regulators and emergency responders.

SUBJECT 04 · 03

Unsafe Conditions and Accident Causation

An unsafe condition exists when a system departs from a state in which hazards are adequately controlled. The condition may result from equipment failure, human action, environmental change, incorrect configuration, poor maintenance or a combination of factors. An accident usually develops through a sequence rather than one isolated mistake.

A useful way to think about causation is as a chain: initiating condition, triggering event, loss of control, exposure and consequence. For example, a maintenance error may leave a fuel connection unsecured. Vibration can then cause leakage. Vapour can accumulate in an enclosed area, an ignition source can appear, and a fire can develop. Each stage creates an opportunity for a barrier to interrupt the sequence.

Accident causation models help engineers avoid blaming the final person who interacted with the system. Organisational decisions, design assumptions, maintenance procedures, training, supervision and environmental conditions can all influence the chain.

Hazard identification should therefore examine both immediate physical mechanisms and the conditions that allow those mechanisms to develop. The objective is to break accident sequences as early as reasonably practicable.

SUBJECT 04 · 04

Sources of Aerospace Hazards

Aerospace hazards originate from several interacting sources: the vehicle, its propulsion and energy systems, the operating environment, people, software, maintenance activities, infrastructure and organisational processes. A complete hazard survey must consider the entire lifecycle rather than only the flight itself.

During design, hazards can arise from inadequate structural margins, incompatible materials, poor human interfaces or single-point failures. During manufacturing, contamination, incorrect assembly and configuration errors may introduce latent defects. During operation, weather, fuel state, workload, traffic and system degradation can create changing exposure conditions. During maintenance, stored energy, incorrect isolation or disturbed systems can introduce new hazards.

The operating environment is especially important. Aircraft encounter pressure changes, turbulence, icing, lightning and temperature variation. Spacecraft face vacuum, radiation, orbital debris and thermal extremes. Launch systems experience intense vibration, acoustic loading, combustion energy and rapidly changing aerodynamic conditions.

Aerospace hazard identification must therefore be lifecycle-based and system-wide. The engineer should consider normal operation, abnormal operation, emergencies, maintenance, transport, storage, disposal and credible combinations of failures rather than assuming that the vehicle is hazardous only when something breaks.

SUBJECT 04 · 05

Physical Hazards in Aerospace Operations

Physical hazards in aerospace operations are hazards that can act directly on people or equipment through mechanical, thermal, electrical, acoustic, pressure, radiation or kinetic mechanisms. Their defining feature is that there is a physical source capable of transferring energy or creating a damaging condition.

Examples include rotating propellers, rotor blades, jet exhaust, hot surfaces, high-pressure fluids, stored electrical energy, compressed gases, intense noise and high-velocity debris. The severity of exposure depends on energy magnitude, distance, duration, shielding and the vulnerability of the target.

A physical hazard can exist even when equipment is functioning normally. An aircraft engine operating correctly produces high-temperature exhaust and substantial acoustic energy. A hydraulic accumulator stores pressure by design. A spacecraft power system contains electrical energy even when there is no fault.

Controls therefore need to account for normal states as well as failures. Physical separation, guarding, interlocks, isolation, shielding, pressure relief, personal protective equipment and administrative restrictions can reduce exposure. The most effective controls generally prevent people from entering the hazardous zone or prevent the hazardous energy from being released.

Understanding the physical mechanism is essential because vague labels such as 'mechanical hazard' do not tell an engineer what can actually cause harm.

SUBJECT 04 · 06

Mechanical and Kinetic-Energy Hazards

Mechanical and kinetic-energy hazards arise when moving masses, rotating components or stored mechanical energy can transfer force to people, structures or other equipment. Aerospace systems contain many such sources: wheels, turbines, compressors, propellers, rotor systems, actuators, landing gear, doors, cargo-handling systems and moving launch equipment.

Kinetic energy increases with the square of velocity, expressed as KE = 1/2 mv². This means that a relatively small mass can become extremely dangerous when moving rapidly. Rotating machinery also stores energy in angular motion, and a component failure can release fragments at high velocity.

Crushing, shearing, entanglement, impact and struck-by hazards are common mechanisms. Rotor blades present additional hazards because their size, speed and aerodynamic forces make the hazard zone difficult to perceive from the ground.

Controls include physical guarding, exclusion zones, interlocks, mechanical stops, chocks, restraints, isolation and verification of zero-energy states before maintenance. Lockout/tagout principles must be adapted to aerospace systems because hydraulic, pneumatic, electrical and gravitational energy may remain stored even after the primary power source is removed.

The safety objective is to prevent unexpected movement and prevent personnel from occupying the path of released mechanical energy.

SUBJECT 04 · 07

Aerodynamic Hazards

Aerodynamic hazards arise from the interaction between an aerospace vehicle and the surrounding airflow. They include stall, spin, turbulence, wind shear, wake turbulence, vortex ring state, rotor downwash, high-speed compressibility effects and excessive aerodynamic loading.

A stall occurs when a wing exceeds its critical angle of attack and loses substantial lift. A spin involves a stalled aircraft in a rotating flight condition and requires specific recovery actions. Wind shear can rapidly change airspeed and flight path, making take-off and landing particularly sensitive. Wake turbulence is generated by aircraft producing lift and can impose strong rolling moments on another aircraft.

Rotorcraft have additional aerodynamic hazards. Vortex ring state can occur when a helicopter descends into its own disturbed rotor flow, reducing effective lift. Loss of tail-rotor effectiveness can create severe yaw-control problems. Rotor downwash can affect people, loose objects and nearby aircraft.

Controls include flight-envelope protection, weather information, separation standards, pilot training, aircraft design, operating procedures and automated warnings. Hazard identification must consider both vehicle-generated airflow and environmental airflow because their interaction can produce conditions not apparent from static inspection.

SUBJECT 04 · 08

Propulsion and Engine Hazards

Propulsion systems contain concentrated energy and operate under high temperature, pressure, speed and vibration. Hazards include engine fire, fuel leakage, compressor or turbine failure, uncontained component failure, overheating, loss of thrust, exhaust hazards, propeller failure and control-system malfunction.

A propulsion failure is not automatically an accident. Its consequence depends on vehicle type, flight phase, redundancy, altitude, terrain, remaining control authority and crew response. A twin-engine aircraft may retain substantial capability after one engine fails, while a single-engine aircraft has a different risk profile. Spacecraft may be unable to recover from a propulsion error if a manoeuvre is time-critical.

Engine containment is an important safety principle. Rotating components can fail at very high speed, and fragments must be prevented from penetrating critical systems or occupied areas. Fire detection and suppression, fuel isolation and thermal protection address other failure modes.

Propulsion hazard analysis should include start-up, shutdown, transient operation, maximum power, abnormal conditions, maintenance and post-failure behaviour. It should also consider interfaces with fuel, electrical, hydraulic, structural and flight-control systems.

The central question is not simply 'can the engine fail?' but 'what happens when it fails, what barriers remain, and can the vehicle reach a safe state?'

SUBJECT 04 · 09

Fuel and Flammable-Fluid Hazards

Fuel and flammable-fluid hazards arise from the chemical and physical properties of fuels, oxidisers and other combustible fluids. Aerospace fuels may be stored in large quantities and distributed through tanks, pumps, valves, pipes and engines. A release can produce vapour, liquid pools, mist or pressurised jets depending on the system.

Combustion requires fuel, an oxidiser and an ignition source in suitable conditions. Removing any one of these elements is a fundamental fire-prevention strategy. However, aerospace systems can contain numerous ignition sources, including hot surfaces, electrical faults, static discharge, engines, friction and lightning.

Fuel vapour is particularly important because vapour concentration can enter a flammable range. Enclosed or poorly ventilated spaces can therefore become hazardous even when the visible amount of liquid is small. Fuel leaks may also migrate along structures and reach ignition sources remote from the original leak.

Controls include fuel-system integrity, leak detection, ventilation, bonding and grounding, ignition-source control, automatic shutoff, fire detection and suppression. Maintenance procedures must address residual fuel, trapped pressure and contamination.

Hazard identification should examine the entire fuel path from storage to combustion and include normal filling, transfer, maintenance, emergency jettison where applicable, crash survivability and post-impact leakage.

SUBJECT 04 · 10

Fire and Explosion Hazards

Fire and explosion hazards are particularly serious in aerospace because aircraft and spacecraft contain fuel, electrical energy, hot surfaces, pressure systems and confined spaces. A fire can rapidly threaten structural integrity, propulsion, avionics and human life.

Fire requires a combustible material, an oxidising environment and an ignition mechanism. An explosion may occur when combustion releases energy rapidly enough to produce damaging pressure or when a confined vapour cloud ignites. The exact mechanism depends on fuel properties, concentration, confinement and ignition conditions.

Aircraft fire protection uses fuel isolation, fire-resistant materials, detection systems, extinguishing agents, ventilation control and structural separation. Engine compartments may contain dedicated fire detection and suppression systems. Cargo compartments may use smoke detection and suppression strategies appropriate to their hazards.

Spacecraft introduce different conditions. Fire behaviour in microgravity can differ from terrestrial fire because buoyancy-driven flow is greatly reduced. Crew vehicles therefore require careful material selection, atmosphere management, detection and response procedures.

Fire hazard identification must consider ignition sources, fuel paths, ventilation, compartment geometry, detection time, suppression capability and the consequences of continued burning. A good design prevents ignition where possible and ensures that a small fire cannot easily become a vehicle-loss event.

SUBJECT 04 · 11

Electrical and Energy Hazards

Electrical and energy hazards occur wherever aerospace systems store, generate, distribute or convert electrical energy. Aircraft may contain high-voltage generation, batteries, wiring networks, actuators and power electronics. Spacecraft can combine high-voltage solar arrays, batteries and tightly controlled power buses.

Electrical shock is one hazard, but aerospace safety also has to consider arc flash, electrical fire, battery thermal runaway, short circuits, electromagnetic interference and loss of power to critical systems. An electrical fault can also create secondary hazards by disabling navigation, flight controls, communications or fire-protection equipment.

Batteries deserve special attention because they store substantial chemical energy. Damage, overcharging, manufacturing defects or thermal abuse can cause internal heating and, in some chemistries, rapid thermal runaway with fire or gas generation.

Controls include insulation, grounding, current limiting, circuit protection, physical separation, fault isolation and monitoring. Critical systems may use independent power sources or redundant distribution paths.

Hazard analysis should trace both the direct consequence of electrical energy and the functional consequence of losing electricity. The most dangerous failure may not be the electrical event itself but the system that becomes unavailable because of it.

SUBJECT 04 · 12

Hydraulic and Pneumatic Hazards

Hydraulic and pneumatic systems use pressurised fluids or gases to transmit power and control mechanical functions. Aircraft use hydraulic systems for flight controls, landing gear, brakes and other high-force functions. Pneumatic systems can provide actuation, environmental control, starting functions and other services.

The stored energy in a pressurised system can be released suddenly if a hose, pipe, fitting or component fails. High-pressure fluid jets can penetrate skin, produce injection injuries or damage nearby equipment. Moving actuators can create crushing and entanglement hazards. Hot hydraulic fluid can also create fire risks if it contacts ignition sources.

Redundancy is widely used in critical aircraft hydraulic architectures. Multiple independent circuits can allow control to continue after a leak or component failure. Isolation valves and accumulators provide additional capability.

Maintenance is a major hazard period because technicians may disturb pressurised systems. Safe procedures require depressurisation, isolation and verification rather than assuming that shutting off a pump has removed all stored energy.

Hazard identification should include pressure sources, stored energy, fluid compatibility, routing near hot components, leak consequences and the effects of losing one or more hydraulic functions.

SUBJECT 04 · 13

Pressure and Pressure-System Hazards

Pressure-system hazards occur when a difference in pressure can produce structural failure, uncontrolled flow, decompression or projectile effects. Aerospace systems contain many pressure boundaries, including aircraft cabins, hydraulic circuits, oxygen systems, fuel tanks, pneumatic systems and spacecraft pressure vessels.

A pressure vessel stores potential energy. If the boundary fails, the stored energy can be released rapidly. In a cabin, decompression can create physiological danger and may also damage equipment or alter the vehicle's structural condition. In a spacecraft, loss of pressure can become immediately life-threatening because there is no breathable external atmosphere.

Pressure hazards can arise from overpressure, underpressure, fatigue cracking, corrosion, manufacturing defects, valve failure, thermal expansion or incorrect configuration. Relief devices, pressure regulation, structural margins, leak detection and isolation are common barriers.

Oxygen systems require particular care because oxygen-rich environments can greatly increase combustion risk. Materials and cleaning processes must therefore be compatible with the intended atmosphere.

Aerospace pressure safety requires understanding both the pressure boundary and what happens after it fails. The hazard analysis should include leak rate, compartment volume, detection time, available isolation and the ability of occupants or systems to reach a safe condition.

SUBJECT 04 · 14

Structural and Material Hazards

Structural and material hazards arise when an aerospace structure cannot carry the loads or environmental stresses imposed on it. Failure mechanisms include fatigue, fracture, corrosion, buckling, delamination, impact damage, thermal degradation and manufacturing defects.

Aircraft structures experience repeated cycles of pressurisation, manoeuvring, landing and vibration. Even when each individual load is below the ultimate strength of the material, repeated cycles can produce fatigue cracks. Damage-tolerant design and inspection programmes are intended to detect or tolerate such degradation before catastrophic failure.

Composite structures introduce different failure mechanisms. Delamination and barely visible impact damage may reduce structural capability without obvious external evidence. Bonded joints and interfaces also require careful control.

Spacecraft structures experience launch vibration, acoustic loads, thermal cycling and sometimes pressure loads. They may also suffer micrometeoroid and debris impacts.

Hazard identification must consider the complete life history of the structure: manufacturing, transportation, operation, maintenance, environmental exposure and ageing. Structural safety is therefore not established only by initial strength testing; it depends on design margins, quality assurance, inspection, monitoring and controlled configuration.

SUBJECT 04 · 15

Vibration, Oscillation and Resonance

Vibration, oscillation and resonance hazards occur when repeated mechanical motion interacts with the natural frequencies of a structure or component. Aerospace vehicles are especially sensitive because engines, rotors, propellers, airflow and control systems can generate significant periodic forces.

Resonance occurs when excitation approaches a system's natural frequency and causes a large response. Excessive vibration can produce fatigue damage, loosen fasteners, degrade electronics, reduce sensor accuracy or make human operation difficult. In rotorcraft, vibration can be associated with rotor imbalance, blade tracking problems or transmission conditions.

Flutter is a particularly important aeroelastic phenomenon in aircraft. It involves interaction between aerodynamic forces, structural flexibility and motion. If uncontrolled, it can develop rapidly and damage the structure.

Spacecraft can experience launch vibration and acoustic excitation that threaten delicate payloads. Components must be qualified for expected vibration environments.

Controls include balancing, damping, stiffness management, vibration isolation, structural redesign, frequency separation and condition monitoring. Hazard identification should examine both the excitation source and the receiving structure because a vibration problem often emerges from their interaction rather than from either component alone.

SUBJECT 04 · 16

Noise and Acoustic Hazards

Noise and acoustic hazards arise from engines, propellers, rotors, airflow, launch vehicles and other high-energy aerospace operations. Noise can cause hearing damage, interfere with communication, increase fatigue and reduce situational awareness.

Aircraft engine noise is particularly significant during ground operations, take-off and maintenance activities. Helicopter rotor noise can affect personnel near operating aircraft. Launch vehicles produce extremely intense acoustic energy that can affect structures and nearby equipment as well as people.

The hazard depends on sound pressure level, frequency, duration, distance and exposure pattern. A short, very intense exposure can present a different risk from prolonged lower-level exposure.

Controls include distance, barriers, acoustic enclosures, operational restrictions, hearing protection and scheduling. Engineering controls are generally preferred because personal protective equipment can interfere with communication and situational awareness.

Hazard identification should therefore include both occupational exposure and functional consequences. A worker who cannot hear a warning because of engine noise may face a secondary safety hazard even if hearing damage itself is avoided.

Noise is consequently both a health hazard and a human-performance hazard.

SUBJECT 04 · 17

Thermal and High-Temperature Hazards

Thermal and high-temperature hazards occur whenever aerospace systems expose people or equipment to temperatures capable of causing burns, material degradation, fire or loss of function. Engines, exhaust systems, brakes, turbines, hot-air ducts and re-entry surfaces are important sources.

High temperature affects material strength and can accelerate oxidation, fatigue and degradation. A component that is structurally adequate at normal temperature may lose significant strength when heated. Thermal expansion can also produce distortion or stress if different materials expand at different rates.

Aircraft braking systems can reach very high temperatures after heavy braking. Engine compartments contain hot components close to fuel and hydraulic systems. High-speed vehicles experience aerodynamic heating, while re-entry vehicles experience extreme thermal loads.

Controls include insulation, thermal barriers, cooling, ventilation, material selection, separation and temperature monitoring. Fire-resistant zones and hot-surface identification help prevent secondary ignition.

Hazard analysis should consider heat transfer mechanisms—conduction, convection and radiation—as well as the duration of exposure. A brief temperature peak and prolonged heating can produce very different consequences.

Thermal safety is therefore closely connected with structural, fire, propulsion and human-exposure hazards.

SUBJECT 04 · 18

Cold and Cryogenic Hazards

Cold and cryogenic hazards arise from very low temperatures and from fluids maintained at cryogenic conditions. Aerospace applications may involve liquid oxygen, liquid hydrogen, liquid methane, helium and other low-temperature substances, particularly in launch systems.

Cryogenic fluids can cause severe cold burns and tissue damage. Rapid vaporisation can also create pressure hazards when liquid expands into gas within confined spaces. Some materials become brittle at low temperature, changing fracture behaviour and seal performance.

Cryogenic oxygen presents an additional fire hazard because oxygen-rich conditions can greatly increase combustion intensity. Hydrogen can form flammable mixtures with air and can migrate into areas where it is difficult to detect.

Launch facilities therefore require specialised ventilation, leak detection, materials compatibility, exclusion zones, pressure relief and controlled transfer procedures.

Cold can also affect ordinary aerospace operations. Very low ambient temperatures can change battery performance, fuel properties, lubrication and material behaviour.

Hazard identification should consider storage, transfer, venting, filling, maintenance and emergency response. The engineer must account for both the low temperature itself and the properties of the fluid after it changes state.

SUBJECT 04 · 19

Radiation Hazards

Radiation hazards in aerospace arise from energetic electromagnetic and particle radiation. Sources include solar radiation, cosmic radiation, trapped radiation belts and, depending on mission and equipment, radioactive materials.

For humans, cumulative radiation exposure can increase long-term health risk, while high-dose exposure can produce acute effects. Spacecraft electronics can experience single-event effects, total ionising dose and displacement damage. These effects can cause temporary faults, permanent degradation or unexpected system behaviour.

Radiation protection may involve shielding, component selection, redundancy, error detection, operational scheduling and spacecraft trajectory planning. No practical spacecraft can eliminate all radiation exposure, so safety engineering often focuses on keeping exposure and failure probability within acceptable limits.

Solar activity is particularly important because energetic particle events can increase radiation levels and affect communications and spacecraft systems.

Hazard identification should consider the mission duration, orbit, shielding, crew exposure, electronic sensitivity and expected space-weather environment.

Radiation is unusual because it can be invisible, cumulative and difficult to detect directly without instrumentation. Safety therefore depends heavily on prediction, monitoring, modelling and design margins rather than visual inspection.

SUBJECT 04 · 20

Vacuum and Low-Pressure Hazards

Vacuum and low-pressure hazards occur when systems or people are exposed to pressures far below normal atmospheric pressure. Spacecraft operate in near-vacuum externally, while aircraft cabins and other pressurised systems may experience pressure loss during operation.

For humans, sudden decompression can cause rapid physiological stress because the body requires an adequate external pressure environment. Reduced oxygen partial pressure can lead to hypoxia, while pressure differences can affect gas-filled body spaces.

Equipment can also be damaged by vacuum. Materials may outgas, lubricants can behave differently and heat transfer changes because convection becomes negligible. Seals, valves and pressure vessels must be specifically designed for the intended environment.

A vacuum hazard can originate from a small leak, a failed pressure boundary, a damaged hatch, valve malfunction or structural penetration. Detection systems may monitor pressure decay or other indicators.

Controls include pressure-vessel design, leak testing, isolation valves, redundant seals, pressure monitoring, protective equipment and emergency procedures.

The key safety question is not only whether a pressure boundary can fail, but how rapidly the environment becomes unsafe and what time is available for detection, isolation and crew response.

SUBJECT 04 · 21

Atmospheric and Weather Hazards

Atmospheric and weather hazards include turbulence, thunderstorms, wind shear, icing conditions, strong winds, heavy precipitation, reduced visibility, temperature extremes and atmospheric pressure variations. These hazards can change rapidly and may be difficult to predict precisely.

Turbulence can produce abrupt changes in aircraft acceleration and load factor. Severe turbulence can injure occupants or damage structures. Wind shear can change the relative wind and aircraft flight path over a short distance, making it especially dangerous near the ground.

Thunderstorms combine multiple hazards: strong updrafts and downdrafts, turbulence, hail, lightning, icing and heavy precipitation. Aircraft normally avoid severe convective weather rather than attempting to penetrate it.

Weather information is therefore a safety barrier. Forecasting, radar, onboard weather systems, pilot reports and operational restrictions allow crews and dispatchers to identify hazardous regions.

Hazard identification must account for both probability and timing. A weather hazard that is tolerable during cruise may become unacceptable during take-off or landing because altitude and recovery options are limited.

Environmental hazard management is therefore an example of dynamic risk assessment: the risk changes continuously as the vehicle moves through different atmospheric conditions.

SUBJECT 04 · 22

Icing and Environmental Contamination

Icing occurs when supercooled water droplets or other environmental contaminants accumulate on an aircraft or its systems. Ice can change the shape of aerodynamic surfaces, increase drag, reduce lift and alter control characteristics. It can also affect engine inlets, sensors, propellers and rotor systems.

The danger is not limited to visible ice. Contamination can accumulate in locations where it is difficult to observe from the cockpit. Small changes in aerodynamic shape can have significant effects when an aircraft is already operating close to performance limits.

Other contamination hazards include snow, slush, dust, volcanic ash and foreign material. Volcanic ash is particularly dangerous to turbine engines because it can enter the engine, melt in hot sections and subsequently solidify on components.

Controls include weather avoidance, de-icing and anti-icing systems, ground inspections, contamination limits and operational procedures. The correct control depends on the material and operating condition.

Hazard identification should examine where contamination can accumulate, what function it affects and whether the crew can detect the degradation before control margins become inadequate. Prevention is often more effective than attempting to recover after performance has already deteriorated.

SUBJECT 04 · 23

Lightning and Electrostatic Hazards

Lightning and electrostatic hazards arise because aerospace vehicles can accumulate electrical charge and can encounter strong electric fields. Aircraft can be struck by lightning during atmospheric flight, while static electricity can develop through friction with air or precipitation.

A lightning strike can produce very high currents and rapid heating. Aircraft are therefore designed with conductive paths and bonding arrangements that help carry current around critical systems rather than through vulnerable components. Fuel systems and electronic equipment require particular attention.

Static electricity can also interfere with communications or create sparks near flammable vapours. Grounding and bonding procedures reduce differences in electrical potential during refuelling and maintenance.

Spacecraft face different charging mechanisms. Interaction with plasma and energetic particles can produce differential charging between surfaces, potentially causing electrostatic discharge and electronic disturbances.

Controls include electrical bonding, conductive materials, lightning protection, static dissipative measures, grounding and careful system layout.

Hazard identification must consider the vehicle's operating environment, fuel or oxidiser exposure, electronic sensitivity and the possible paths for electrical current. The objective is to control both the source of charge and the consequences of an unwanted discharge.

SUBJECT 04 · 24

Human and Physiological Hazards

Human and physiological hazards arise because aerospace environments can exceed the normal limits of human physiology. Important factors include acceleration, hypoxia, pressure changes, temperature, vibration, noise, fatigue, radiation, motion sickness and limited access to food, water or medical support.

Acceleration can produce high loads on the body, particularly during launch, manoeuvring or abrupt aircraft motion. The direction of acceleration matters because the cardiovascular system responds differently to different loading orientations.

Reduced atmospheric pressure can produce hypoxia and decompression-related problems. Long-duration spaceflight introduces microgravity-related physiological changes and radiation exposure. High workload and sleep disruption can reduce human performance even when the physical environment remains within nominal limits.

Human physiological hazards are therefore both direct and indirect. Fatigue may not injure a person by itself, but it can increase the probability of a navigation, maintenance or decision-making error.

Controls include environmental control systems, crew restraints, oxygen systems, pressure protection, thermal control, workload management, medical monitoring, exercise and operational limitations.

Aerospace safety must treat the human as part of the system rather than as an external variable. Vehicle design, procedures and emergency systems must reflect actual human capabilities and limitations.

SUBJECT 04 · 25

Human-Error and Human-Factors Hazards

Human-error hazards occur when a person's action, omission, interpretation or decision contributes to an unsafe condition. In aerospace systems, human error should not automatically be interpreted as carelessness. The design of the task, interface, workload, training, procedures and organisational environment strongly influences human performance.

Errors can include slips, lapses, mistakes, incorrect assumptions, mode confusion and communication failures. A pilot may select the wrong control because two controls look similar. A maintainer may install a component incorrectly because identification is ambiguous. An operator may make a reasonable decision based on incomplete or misleading information.

Human factors engineering attempts to design systems that make correct actions easier and errors less likely or less consequential. Clear displays, intuitive controls, independent verification, checklists, training and automation design are examples.

A resilient system does not assume that people will never make mistakes. It provides barriers that detect or contain mistakes before they produce catastrophic consequences.

Hazard identification should therefore examine task demands, time pressure, workload, communication, interfaces, procedures and recovery opportunities. The safety question is not simply 'who made the error?' but 'why was the error possible, and why did the system allow it to propagate?'

SUBJECT 04 · 26

Avionics, Software and Control-System Hazards

Avionics, software and control systems are central to modern aerospace vehicles and can create hazards when they produce incorrect outputs, lose availability or behave unexpectedly. A software failure may not damage hardware directly, but it can command an actuator, engine or flight-control surface incorrectly.

Safety-critical systems use requirements engineering, verification, validation, redundancy, monitoring and controlled configuration. Independent channels can compare sensor data or control commands. Watchdogs and fault-detection systems can identify abnormal behaviour and place equipment into predefined states.

Software hazards are difficult because software does not wear out in the same way as a mechanical component. The dominant risks often involve incorrect requirements, design defects, interface assumptions, timing errors, unexpected combinations of inputs or configuration changes.

Automation can reduce workload but can also create mode-awareness problems. A crew may believe the system is operating in one control mode while the computer is actually following another logic path.

Hazard identification must therefore consider functional failure, incorrect function and unintended function. Engineers need to ask what happens if a system stops, what happens if it gives the wrong answer, and what happens if it performs a valid action at the wrong time.

SUBJECT 04 · 27

Communication, Navigation and Surveillance Hazards

Communication, navigation and surveillance systems provide information required to know where a vehicle is, where other vehicles are and what instructions apply. Their failure can create hazards ranging from loss of situational awareness to incorrect trajectory or collision.

Navigation systems can suffer from sensor failures, signal loss, interference, incorrect databases or software errors. Communication systems can lose connectivity because of equipment failure, antenna orientation, atmospheric conditions, interference or excessive distance. Surveillance systems can provide incomplete or incorrect information about surrounding traffic.

A critical distinction is between loss of information and misleading information. A visible system failure may prompt an operator to use a backup, whereas an incorrect but plausible output can remain unnoticed and drive inappropriate decisions.

Controls include redundant sensors, independent navigation sources, cross-checking, integrity monitoring, backup communication methods and defined degraded modes.

Space missions add long communication delays and periods of limited contact. Spacecraft must therefore possess autonomous fault detection and safe-state capabilities.

Hazard identification should trace what decisions depend on each information source and what happens if the information becomes unavailable, delayed, corrupted or incorrect.

SUBJECT 04 · 28

Ground-Handling and Ramp Hazards

Ground-handling and ramp operations expose aerospace personnel to hazards that exist before flight. Aircraft can move unexpectedly, engines can produce dangerous exhaust, vehicles can collide, ground equipment can strike structures and personnel can be exposed to fuel, electricity, noise or rotating machinery.

Common hazards include jet blast, propeller and rotor zones, aircraft towing, pushback, refuelling, baggage handling, elevated work, vehicle movement and equipment positioning. The risk is often increased because many different organisations operate around the same aircraft.

Ground operations also involve frequent configuration changes. Doors, panels, landing gear, engines, external power connections and fuelling systems may be in unusual states. Poor communication can result in an aircraft being moved while personnel or equipment remain in a hazardous position.

Controls include marked exclusion zones, marshalling procedures, chocks, wheel restraints, engine operating restrictions, communication protocols, lighting, ground-support equipment inspections and competency requirements.

Hazard identification should map the physical movement of people, aircraft and vehicles rather than looking only at individual tasks. Interfaces between teams are particularly important because responsibility can become ambiguous during fast-paced turnaround operations.

SUBJECT 04 · 29

Maintenance and Maintenance-Induced Hazards

Maintenance is a major source of aerospace hazards because technicians intentionally disturb systems that are normally configured for safe operation. Panels are opened, energy sources are isolated, components are removed, wiring is disconnected and protective barriers may temporarily be defeated.

Maintenance-induced hazards include incorrect installation, foreign-object damage, incomplete reassembly, tool or material contamination, wiring errors, incorrect software configuration and failure to restore a system to its operational state.

Stored energy is especially important. Electrical, hydraulic, pneumatic, thermal, gravitational and mechanical energy can remain present after the main system is switched off. Maintenance procedures therefore require identification and isolation of energy sources followed by verification.

Independent inspection and functional testing provide additional barriers. Configuration control ensures that the correct parts, software versions and technical instructions are used.

Human factors are also significant. Maintenance often occurs under time pressure and may involve repetitive tasks. Poor access, ambiguous instructions or inadequate lighting can increase error probability.

Hazard identification should include the task as performed, not merely the task as described in a manual. Engineers should examine what can be disturbed, what can be left disconnected, what can be installed incorrectly and how the error could remain hidden until flight.

SUBJECT 04 · 30

Launch and Ascent Hazards

Launch and ascent combine enormous stored energy with rapidly changing aerodynamic and structural conditions. Major hazards include propulsion failure, fire, explosion, structural breakup, guidance failure, debris generation, excessive vibration, acoustic loading and loss of control.

Before ignition, launch vehicles may contain large quantities of hazardous propellants. During ascent, engines operate at extreme temperature and pressure while the vehicle accelerates rapidly. The vehicle passes through regions of changing aerodynamic pressure and may experience severe dynamic loads.

Range safety systems and flight termination systems may be used to prevent an uncontrolled vehicle from threatening populated areas. Ground crews require controlled exclusion zones and strict propellant-handling procedures.

Guidance and propulsion are closely coupled. A small deviation early in flight can become significant as the vehicle accelerates. Redundant sensors, control logic, telemetry and independent monitoring therefore play important roles.

Launch hazard identification must cover the entire sequence from vehicle preparation and fuelling through ignition, ascent, staging and early orbital insertion. Ground personnel, the launch vehicle, payload, infrastructure and surrounding public must all be considered as potential exposed populations or assets.

SUBJECT 04 · 31

Orbital, Spacecraft and Space-Environment Hazards

Orbital and spacecraft hazards arise from the long-duration operation of a vehicle in an environment that is inaccessible for routine physical maintenance. Important hazards include orbital debris, micrometeoroids, radiation, vacuum, thermal extremes, loss of attitude control, power failure, communication loss and propulsion errors.

Orbital debris can collide with spacecraft at very high relative velocity. Even a small object can puncture shielding or damage critical systems. Tracking networks and collision-avoidance manoeuvres reduce the probability, while impact-resistant shielding can reduce consequences.

Loss of attitude control can affect solar-array pointing, antenna alignment, thermal balance and propulsion orientation. A spacecraft may remain structurally intact but gradually lose its ability to generate power or communicate.

Long-duration operation also introduces ageing and degradation. Batteries lose capacity, materials experience thermal cycling and electronics can accumulate radiation damage.

Spacecraft safety therefore relies heavily on redundancy, fault detection, autonomous safe modes, ground monitoring and carefully planned contingency procedures. Hazard analysis must consider not only immediate failure but also slow degradation and combinations of failures that may interact over months or years.

SUBJECT 04 · 32

Re-entry, Descent and Landing Hazards

Re-entry, descent and landing combine high energy, aerodynamic heating, structural loading and precise control. A vehicle returning from orbit must first manage its trajectory so that it encounters the atmosphere at an appropriate speed, angle and location.

During atmospheric entry, aerodynamic forces increase rapidly. The vehicle experiences heating, deceleration and changing stability characteristics. Thermal protection must remain effective while guidance maintains the desired trajectory.

During descent, parachutes, aerodynamic surfaces, engines or combinations of these systems may be used depending on the vehicle. Landing introduces additional hazards such as terrain, wind, structural impact loads and propulsion-system failure.

The final phase often has very little time or altitude available for recovery. A fault that would be manageable during cruise may become catastrophic during landing because the vehicle cannot climb away or divert.

Hazard identification should therefore be phase-specific. Engineers must examine entry guidance, thermal protection, attitude control, deployment mechanisms, landing propulsion, landing gear or impact structures, navigation and terrain hazards.

Re-entry and landing safety depend on maintaining adequate margins throughout a rapidly changing environment and ensuring that critical failures have credible detection and recovery paths.

SUBJECT 04 · 33

Hazard Identification Methods

Hazard identification is the systematic process of finding credible sources of harm before they produce an accident. It begins with defining the system, mission, operating environment, interfaces and intended functions. Without a clear system boundary, important hazards can be missed.

Methods include structured checklists, preliminary hazard analysis, design reviews, task analysis, what-if analysis, functional hazard analysis, FMEA, fault-tree analysis and operating experience. Different methods reveal different types of problems.

A good identification process asks what can go wrong, what can cause it, what can be exposed, what happens next and which barriers already exist. It should examine normal operation, abnormal conditions, emergencies, maintenance and credible combinations of failures.

Hazard identification should involve people who understand the real system, including designers, operators, maintainers and specialists. Operational experience often reveals hazards that are difficult to predict from drawings alone.

The output should be specific enough to support analysis. 'Engine hazard' is too broad. 'Uncontained turbine-fragment release penetrating a critical control-system region' describes a mechanism that can be analysed.

Hazard identification is therefore the beginning of safety analysis, not the final safety assessment.

SUBJECT 04 · 34

Hazard Analysis and Classification

Hazard analysis develops identified hazards into structured descriptions of causes, consequences, likelihood, severity and controls. Classification helps engineers organise hazards according to their source, operating phase, affected function or consequence.

Severity categories describe the magnitude of possible consequences, while likelihood or probability describes how often a hazardous event may occur. Different aerospace organisations and regulatory systems use different classification schemes, so the definitions must always be taken from the applicable standard or programme.

Functional hazard analysis asks what happens when an aircraft or spacecraft function is lost, degraded or incorrectly performed. FMEA examines components or functions and traces failure modes to effects. Fault-tree analysis works backward from a defined top event to combinations of causes.

The most useful analysis is specific. Instead of asking whether 'navigation is unsafe,' an engineer might analyse loss of navigation integrity during a critical phase of flight and determine what independent information remains available.

Classification should never become a substitute for engineering judgement. A numerical risk category is meaningful only when the underlying assumptions, failure mechanisms and exposure conditions are understood.

SUBJECT 04 · 35

Hazard Controls, Barriers and Mitigation

Hazard controls are the barriers that prevent hazardous conditions from becoming accidents or reduce the consequences when prevention fails. The strongest controls generally act on the hazard itself or prevent exposure rather than relying entirely on human action.

Engineering controls include containment, physical separation, automatic shutdown, interlocks, redundancy, shielding, pressure relief, fire suppression and fault detection. Administrative controls include procedures, training, permits, operating limits, checklists and restrictions. Personal protective equipment is normally a later barrier for occupational exposure because it depends on correct selection and use.

Aerospace systems often use multiple independent barriers because no single control is assumed to be perfect. A fuel fire-control strategy may combine leak prevention, ignition-source control, detection, isolation and suppression. A spacecraft pressure system may combine structural margins, leak detection, isolation and emergency crew procedures.

Barrier independence matters. Two controls that depend on the same sensor, power source or human action may fail together and therefore provide less protection than their apparent number suggests.

Hazard control should follow the hazard mechanism. The best control is one that interrupts the accident sequence early, is reliable, can be verified and remains effective under credible failures.

SUBJECT 04 · 36

From Hazard Identification to Aerospace Safety

Aerospace safety is the result of connecting fundamentals, hazard identification, analysis, controls and operational assurance into one continuous process. The safety engineer begins by understanding the vehicle and its mission, identifies credible hazards, analyses accident sequences, selects barriers and then verifies that those barriers remain effective.

Hazard identification is not a one-time activity performed before certification. Design changes, new missions, software updates, maintenance findings, incidents and changes in operating environment can introduce new hazards or alter existing risk.

A mature safety process therefore maintains a living hazard record. Each significant hazard has a defined description, causes, consequences, controls, verification evidence, responsible owner and status. Changes are assessed for their effect on existing assumptions and safety barriers.

The final objective is not to eliminate every hazard. Aerospace systems contain unavoidable hazards such as high energy, propulsion, pressure and environmental exposure. The objective is to control them so that credible accident sequences are prevented or mitigated to an acceptable level under the applicable safety criteria.

This subject therefore establishes the foundation for later work in risk assessment, FMEA, fault trees, human factors, fire safety, emergency planning, accident investigation and aerospace safety management. The engineer moves from asking 'what can hurt us?' to asking 'how do we know it is controlled?'
